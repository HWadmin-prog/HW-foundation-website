<p>The turning off of LongWave, the moment when the transmitters at Droitwich near Birmingham in the UK’s midlands finally fell silent, got me thinking about the nature of obsolescence. Anyone who has a longwave radio will now only receive static. That radio is now, to all intents and purposes, functionally obsolete. It doesn’t work anymore. The same can be said for items such as modems or packet switch data network devices, they all no longer function because the infrastructure that enabled their function is simply no longer there. This type of obsolescence is very binary. It either works, or it doesn’t.</p>

    <p>We are surrounded by obsolescence. It is one of the defining characteristics of our age. As consumers, we are used to the concept of buying the latest version of things, the latest fad, or if the Yorkshireman is strong it us, hanging on to something until it is worn out, however, the Oct 2025 withdrawal of support of WiFi support for Google Nest thermostats illustrated to consumers something that the commercial sector has suffered for years and suffers in ever growing volumes, premature obsolescence. The purposeful withdrawal of support for a product or system by a manufacturer that then necessitates the user to have to replace it, often at great expense. Why? to maintain that manufacturers turnover and profits.</p>

    <h2>Premature obsolescence has huge impacts. </h2>
    <p>Firstly, it costs money to replace products and systems, but not just in purchasing and engineering them, it costs management time to find the money to do it, to organise shut downs, which in themselves result in lost throughput or production. Staff need training on new systems. Furthermore, obsolescence in itself can create a productivity drag. Why make improvements on the old system when you have to rip it out in 3 years’ time because it is “obsolete”. 3 years becomes 5, because the money wasn’t available, meaning 5 years of lost productivity or cost savings. Obsolete products have to disposed of, leading to a waste problem and their new replacements will need a whole host of critical minerals and commodities to make, many of them with geo-political implications. Western democracies are strapped for cash and would rather be spending it upon defence.</p>


    <h2> Yet what actually is obsolescence? </h2>
    <p>The opening paragraph described the clear cut binary of functional obsolescence. But when you analyse many other systems and items that we now regard as obsolete, or that manufacturers tell us are obsolete, the functional obsolescence described above does not actually apply to them. Therefore, other forms of obsolescence must exist otherwise we would still be using typewriters and steam locomotives. What are these other forms of obsolescence and are they analogue in nature rather than the binary functional obsolescence described above? </p>
   
    <h2>Lets take a coach and horses</h2>
    <p>for example. You could still use the coach and horses to travel from Bristol to London, i.e, it isn’t actually functionally obsolete, however, none of the infrastructure that is needed, for example, fresh horses at regular intervals, water troughs for the horses to drink from, no longer exist and while it may be possible to travel by coach and horse from Bristol to London, logistically it would be nigh on impossible. So we would describe the obsolescence of the coach and horse as being logistical obsolescence. And it’s logistical obsolescence would be very high because hardly any of the infrastructure that is required for it is available. If however we took a diesel railway locomotive built in the 1960s, obviously it isn’t functionally obsolete. It is still perfectly capable of hauling a significantly sized train at reasonable line speeds. However, the people trained to drive it and the spares for its engines would be difficult but not impossible to obtain. It’s logistical obsolescence is therefore low but obviously still significant in the decision as to whether to use it as a mainline railway locomotive.  </p>


  <p> There are then items that appear not to have either functional obsolescence or even logistical obsolescence. For example, take an ocean liner. It is clearly not functionally obsolete in that it could sail from Southampton to New York and on arrival at either of those ports it could be fuelled with bunker oil and stored with the necessary provisions for its voyage. Jetties exist that it could moor up alongside and embark and disgorge its passengers. So if it is not functionally obsolete and it’s logistical obsolescence is low then what rails against its wider use? This would appear to be its commercial obsolescence. Someone can fly from London to New York in four hours rather than taking 10 days so the ocean liner is simply not commercially viable when compared with other options, therefore it is commercially obsolete. Furthermore, even if a very limited market existed for people crossing the Atlantic by ocean liner, it simply wouldn’t be economic to operate a vessel the size of an ocean liner for maybe only a handful of paying passengers. Commercial obsolescence is again an analogue and clearly in the example above, The ocean liners commercial obsolescence is very high, possibly 99%. The example given above of the railway locomotive built in the 1960s also suffers from commercial obsolescence, hence why there are not large numbers of locomotives around from the 1960s for the obvious reason that it’s power and maximum line speed would mean that the intercity trains it hauled would not be competitive with current generations of intercity trains or other forms of transport such as air travel or motor transport. However, in the case the railway locomotive the commercial obsolescence is much lower because under certain circumstances, it would still be commercially viable to operate, for example, there are still a small number of railway locomotives built in the 1960s that are used on sleeper services to England’s West Country. </p>

  <h2>Types of Obsolescence</h2>
  <p> There is a further form of obsolescence which I have labelled safety and security obsolescence. Again this is an analogue and while there may be an immediate impact of something being safety or security obsolete, there is a strong possibility that the necessary safety or security systems can be retrofitted to it so that it can still be used. For example, a railway locomotive from the 1960s may need to be fitted with a modern train protection system to allow it to continue to operate in which case its safety or security obsolescence would be relatively low because while there would be a cost to this retrofit, it would be cost effective to do if it allowed that locomotive to continue operating. On the other hand the safety obsolescence could be far more fundamental, for example a Mk1 British railway carriage built in the 1950s does not have sufficient crash worthiness to be allowed to operate on a modern railway network and the costs involved in bringing it up to current crash worthiness standards would involve such a fundamental rebuild of the rail vehicle as to be uneconomic. Hence again you can see that safety and security obsolescence is an analogue with some systems having a low percentage and other items having a high percentage as can be seen from the above example.  </p>

  <p> The retrofit of more modern equipment into older products or systems can also reduce commercial or logistical obsolescence. In the example above, railway locomotives from the 1960s and 1970s have been fitted with new diesel engines and alternators and their associated electrical & control systems. This has increased power output and reduced running costs, reducing the commercial and logistical obsolescence percentages.  </p>

  <p> By this measure, you could of course argue that functional obsolescence doesn’t exist. Anything can be retrofitted to eliminate functional obsolescence. However, it you gutted the inside of a LW radio and converted it to DAB, then it would no longer be a LW radio, it would just be a radio and we haven’t declared radios to be obsolete.  </p>


  <p> Fundamentally, very few items or systems are actually functionally obsolete in the simple binary sense that they can no longer operate at all. They will have varying degrees of logistical, commercial and safety obsolescence and arguably can have these very soon after being set to work because some industries and technologies, at certain times, move quickly. However, they aren’t functionally obsolete and their other forms of obsolescence can be managed, for example, by retrofitting new systems to them. Therefore if a manufacturer says to you such and such a system is obsolete, they need to qualify what they mean. The various levels of its obsolescence need to be understood so that an informed decision can be made as to whether it is replaced in its entirety or undergoes a series of upgrades which diminishes all three of the above obsolescence’s. In addition, logistical obsolescence can also be reduced by insuring that the logistical support for an item or system remains throughout its useful life. Motor cars are a very good example of this providing large numbers of them are still in existence. The skills to maintain them and the parts with which to maintain them will still be readily available.</p>

  <p> This brings us to the iniquitous relationship between the manufacturer and their products. Manufacturers are fundamental to the logistical support of many modern systems with their software licensing, closed communications protocols and often proprietary software and firmware. The manufacturer can withdraw support for these and by withholding design data from the systems clients, the client, or anyone else for that matter, cannot support the system. Manufacturers can push the logistical obsolescence % to a point where the replacement of the system is the only option.  </p>

  <p> This reflects the increasing deposition of system manufacturers to seek rent through obsolescence, rather than through innovating their products or seeking new markets. Commercial organisations have divined that driving their sales through obsolescence is a far lower cost and more assured approach to maintaining their turnover and profits. The nature of systems, whereby their centre of gravity lies in their proprietary protocols, software and firmware, rather than products, such as a railway locomotive, where their centre of gravity exists in their physicality, make them far easier for the manufacturer to impose obsolescence upon. This imposes financial, management and business interruption costs upon the client. </p>


  <p> Of course, an item or system may also be worn out through use and hence beyond economic repair. Also, its failure rate be so high that its failure, even with spares and support available, poses a risk to critical systems. This would only apply to a small number of systems or those engaged in life safety applications such as fire alarm systems. The broad swathe of systems can be supported on an ongoing basis and their failure managed.</p>

  <p>As can be seen, apart from a few exceptional cases, obsolescence isn’t binary. It is actually a complex interrelationship of analogue values which reflect commercial changes, changes in the support ecosystems around products and systems, increased security challenges and improvements in safety systems. The above hopefully illustrates that genuine obsolescence is few and far between. Without binary obsolescence, the various obsolescence quotients of most systems have the ability to be managed, hence allowing the system to deliver anything from a satisfactory to a best of class performance with suitable upgrades and enhancements.</p>

<h2>I hope this gives clients an intellectual toolbox with which to tackle premature obsolescence and demand a more client focused approach from their suppliers than is often the case these days.</h2>
